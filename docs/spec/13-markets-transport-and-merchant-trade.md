# 13. Markets, transport and merchant trade

## 13.1 Multiple local markets

Goods have local prices and inventories. Settlements and regional market areas resolve supply, demand, reservation needs, affordability and sales. Market demand comes from households, businesses, institutions, projects and armies. Trade adjusts to expected price differences after costs, not direct ruler omniscience.

Prices should reflect scarcity and access while changing smoothly enough to avoid numerical instability. Markets do not have to model every bilateral haggle; periodic aggregated clearing is acceptable. The crown sees market estimates, not absolute knowledge of every hidden stockpile abroad.

## 13.2 Movement graph

Economic travel runs on a network of settlements, crossroads, roads, bridges, mountain trails, navigable rivers, ports and sea lanes. Every edge has travel time, carrying capacity, maintenance/condition, cost, seasonality, security and controlling rights/tolls. Geography determines viable links. Waterways can move heavy bulk commodities efficiently, but upstream movement, river depth and seasonal conditions matter. Dangerous or blocked roads can redirect trade to longer alternatives.

Goods dispatched physically leave the origin inventory, become **in transit**, and enter destination inventory only on successful delivery. Losses, delay and seizure are recorded. Inventories must not be counted simultaneously at source, vehicle and destination. On handover, ownership and tax obligations update according to actual trade terms.

## 13.3 Merchant autonomy

Merchant groups and private traders seek profitable routes according to expected local prices, capital, security, access rights and capacity. Routine flows are aggregated network shipments; strategically important military supply caravans, vulnerable cargo and major trading fleets may be promoted into visible world entities. Do not animate every cart globally. Trade specialization, middlemen and wholesale warehouses emerge from route profitability and institutional support.

## 13.4 Trade policy and diplomacy

The ruler may grant market charters, reduce tolls, fund roads, secure routes, negotiate trade agreements, impose embargoes or commandeer shipping within lawful capacity. Toll revenue competes with trade volume; confiscations create political and merchant consequences. Border agreements and wars change access. An embargo cannot erase physical stockpiles; smuggling and alternate routes may exist, with difficulty and risk.

## 13.5 Roads, rivers and ports

Buildable roads follow terrain, grades, soil stability, river crossings and cost. Maintenance consumes labor/materials. Roads degrade faster under heavy traffic, rain and poor construction; impassability depends on condition, drainage and season. Bridges have spans, weight/traffic capacity, structural condition and defensibility. River transport requires navigability and ports/landing sites. Ships have cargo, crew, weather constraints and maritime risks.

## 13.6 Trade shocks and explanation

A bridge destroyed in battle alters shortest viable routes, shipment arrival times, local prices and supply. A flooded road raises costs. Pirates or raiders cause risk and losses. UI causal reporting should identify disrupted link, affected goods, affected markets and available interventions. Price warnings must not say merely “−17% trade” without explaining why.

## 13.7 No universal shared stockpile

A kingdom-wide resources panel may sum inventories, but such totals are **informational only**. Spending locally still requires physical availability and transport. Contracts may reserve goods not yet delivered, but reservation is not actual supply. Emergency redistribution takes time. This applies equally to construction sites, besieged towns and active armies.

### Borders, right of passage and war-disrupted logistics

Ordinary commerce, troop passage, requisition rights, customs rights, naval access and permission to establish depots are distinct treaty capabilities. An ally is not automatically entitled to march an army, garrison a town, commandeer goods or collect local taxes. A friendly army passing on an authorized route leaves local property and government intact. Unauthorized military crossing triggers enforceable diplomatic demands, interception, reputation changes and possibly hostilities; it does not spontaneously annex the route. Blockade and patrols obstruct **specific usable transport links and capacities**, including river or maritime alternatives, rather than instantly cutting off every destination inside a drawn loop. Political and military access must be incorporated into route planning, merchant forecasts and army supply.

## 13.8 Transport edges are environmental consumers

Roads and river routes consume exact **slope, surface material, soil saturation, snow/ice, channel discharge, ford depth/current, bridge condition and wind/water exposure**. A merchant shipment and a campaigning army may have different vehicle/body constraints, but cannot disagree about whether a bridge exists or whether a river is swollen. When a real flood blocks a route, the transport system re-evaluates capacity, delivery time and alternatives; a subsequent market shortage follows from failed deliveries, not an independently randomized `trade disruption` event.

Weather can change route conditions during transit. River navigation depends on channel depth, current, water level and vessel limits; near-shore or open-sea trade also consumes suitable modeled wind and storm conditions at strategic abstraction.
