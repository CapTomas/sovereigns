# 21. Territorial sovereignty, military control, passage and encirclement

Territory is not captured by crossing a colored boundary or completing a geometric loop. The player expands influence through **physically credible military presence**, control of settlements and routes, negotiated submission and ultimately political settlement. The result should feel spatially satisfying—frontiers move as armies secure the countryside—without rewarding implausible cavalry scribbles. This is a **foundational campaign system**, not a cosmetic overlay.

## 21.1 Six separate territorial questions

For any location the world must answer, without collapsing the answers into a single owner identifier:

1. **Claim:** which authorities assert rights over the land, population or title? Rival claims may coexist.
2. **Legal sovereignty:** which political power is recognized in the relevant legal order as the ruler? Sovereignty may be disputed.
3. **Jurisdiction and property:** which lord, town, community, religious institution or crown domain has administrative, tenancy, tax and property rights? Landownership is not identical to sovereignty.
4. **Effective military control:** which force can actually secure the location, deny hostile passage and enforce immediate orders, and at what strength and certainty?
5. **Access rights:** which particular factions may lawfully transit, supply, trade, station forces, raid, patrol or establish depots under treaties and current war relations?
6. **Knowledge:** which of the above has each faction actually observed or inferred? The simulation knows truth; the player may not.

The player interface must never represent a temporary raid as permanent annexation. Occupation can exist in a sovereign's legal territory without transferring title; an ally's permitted army can operate there without acquiring jurisdiction.

## 21.2 Military presence, reach and zones of influence

An army projects **potential reach** over traversable surrounding routes, based on unit mobility, reconnaissance, terrain, passability, daylight, weather and supply. Potential reach means capacity to threaten or respond; it is **not** yet secured territory. The army can actually hold ground only through its current position, sufficiently frequent patrols, controlled infrastructure, stationed detachments, compliant local communities and defensible supply links.

A fort, watchpost, garrison, town militia or mobile army contributes a particular **security and interdiction capacity** to its reachable network. Route constraints, patrol travel times, choke points, local resistance, force condition and hostile competitors determine how that capacity is distributed. Rugged valleys, impassable mountains, wide rivers, fortified towns and hostile woods break or narrow influence even when their straight-line distance is small. Crossing a road junction may exert disproportionate leverage because connected routes depend upon it; crossing an empty hilltop need not.

Show military presence as three understandable states rather than falsely precise colored circles:

- **Secured:** a force or functioning authority can generally enforce immediate local military decisions and keep the important routes open for itself.
- **Contested:** rival forces, active resistance, insecure roads or inadequate patrols prevent sustained uncontested enforcement.
- **Threatened / traversed:** enemy troops have been seen, raided or passed through, but the attacker lacks maintained occupation.

Control is **time dependent**. A marching column does not instantaneously paint a thick ribbon of ownership. Security grows where detachments establish watch, fortifications submit and local routes are patrolled; it decays when forces leave, provisions fail, passes close or local defenders regain access. A location can be threatened by more than one force while being firmly held by neither. Do not use a universal numerical radius and do not let terrain become a purely cosmetic influence map.

## 21.3 Deliberate occupation and securing territory

An army may receive a **Secure Area** order identifying a route corridor, crossing, settlement cluster or region of interest. The commander attempts to seize necessary sites, establish defensible positions, send patrols, seek submission, protect deliveries, suppress immediate hostile movement and leave garrisons as authorized. Orders are constrained by personnel, time, political authority, actual terrain and available supplies. The player chooses objectives and priorities; the simulation determines their credible extent and duration. The game explains if the army is too small to secure the requested area.

An undefended village may submit after an approaching enemy demonstrates credible force; local acceptance depends on authority, reputation, existing obligations, fear, interests and resistance. Submission saves the effort of an unnecessary battle, but doesn't erase local claims or make all goods free to requisition. Walled towns and occupied castles retain their own resistance capability; their surrender requires negotiation, blockade, assault or some other credible transition. Civilians are never simply converted into soldiers or tax-paying loyalists by a map-color transition.

Secure territory is costly. Garrisons, patrols, camp upkeep, roads, stores, watch posts, administrators and negotiated cooperation sustain it. The occupying force may relocate onward if it establishes sustainable local control behind it. If it fails to do so, a vacated district can become contested or revert to defenders without requiring an explicit counter-conquest battle at every field.

## 21.4 Passage, alliance, neutrality and trespass

**Military access is distinct from political friendship and ownership.** Legal access is tested for the particular faction, location, action and time. Treaty capabilities may separately include transit by land, port access, basing, rights to buy supplies, permitted depot construction, patrol jurisdiction and right to engage enemies while on host territory. Trade access does not automatically include military access.

| Situation | Army can move? | Territorial consequence | Diplomatic consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Own territory under effective jurisdiction | Yes, if physically passable | No sovereignty change; army may reinforce local security | Normal domestic command |
| Ally granting military transit | Yes, within agreement | Host retains ownership, tax rights and control unless otherwise agreed | Treaty rights and obligations apply |
| Ally without military access | Not as authorized transit; may seek consent | No conquest merely from crossing | Unauthorized crossing is a violation |
| Neutral granting limited transit | Yes, within scope, potentially while neutrality continues | No annexation or occupation | Neutrality preserved unless further actions violate it |
| Neutral refusing or lacking access | Physically possible but unlawful trespass, subject to resistance | No automatic ownership change | Protest, ultimatum, interception, war risk |
| Enemy at war | Enter by viable route subject to defenders | Threat, contested presence or secured occupation according to actual conduct | Hostile incursion; combat can occur |
| Truce or peace with withdrawal terms | Only routes and deadlines allowed by agreement | Continued unauthorized garrison can prolong disputed occupation | Breach damages settlement and may renew war |
| Vassal or semi-autonomous territory | Depends on actual charter and feudal obligations | Rights remain with local institutions except specified royal powers | Disputes over levies and movement are political events |

An order that passes through nonfriendly territory must show the applicable legal status before execution. The player can request permission, reroute, issue a knowingly violating order or abandon movement. Trespass is not automatically equivalent to instant formal war: the offended ruler can respond with demands, escorts, denial, seizure or military escalation. A trespassing army can be intercepted and may trigger combat if either side chooses to fight. Under no circumstances does an access violation become a zero-cost exploit or accidental stealth annexation.

## 21.5 Conquest is secured power, not geometry

Marching around a loop and reconnecting to friendly land **never captures the enclosed region by itself**. The campaign can visually show newly threatened corridors and expanding secured footholds, but political boundaries do not automatically fill the loop. What matters is whether relevant settlements, bridges, fords, roads, ports, fortresses and population centers can actually be held and supplied.

A small mobile force can raid or threaten a large area, but cannot permanently govern it. A substantial force can secure open countryside and obtain surrender of unfortified settlements without fighting every village. Strongholds, hostile garrisons and active defenders continue projecting resistance even when encircled. A captured strategic bridge may extend operational influence into adjacent valleys; this is a consequence of reachability, not an abstract adjacency bonus.

Each location's transition occurs when a credible event changes enforcement: arrival, engagement, surrender, fortified capture, local defection, withdrawal, supply failure, treaty change or organized revolt. Record meaningful transitions in campaign history and allow them to be reversed through later actions.

## 21.6 Encirclement and severing access

Encirclement is a **network and capacity problem**, not a geometric polygon-fill problem. A besieger closes or materially restricts the usable roads, crossings, river channels, passes, ports and off-road avenues through which a defended area can receive people, food, messages and military help. A drawn ring on the map is only an explanatory visualization; it has no independent power.

Isolation is evaluated against actual reachable paths and throughput. Armies and forts can interdict links based on range, reaction time, patrol density, defensive terrain, naval or river capability, local informants, season and operational supplies. An interrupted road doesn't imply that a navigable river or mountain path also stops working. A narrow valley with three blocked passes can be effectively isolated without a circular army maneuver; a large open plain with a barely patrolled circle may remain permeable.

Encirclement affects stored supplies, trade arrivals, military replacements, communication and civilian options. The isolated population and garrison consume **existing inventories** over time; hunger, attrition, morale, negotiated surrender and breakout pressure emerge from the duration and completeness of the blockade. A defender with stocks, local fields, wells, smugglers, friendly ports or relief forces may survive for a long time. Air-tight instant starvation and automatic territory transfer are excluded.

The attacker also requires supplies and sufficient troops to maintain the blockade; spreading thinly over many crossings can expose detachments to defeat. Enemy sortie, counterattack, weather, reopened ford, thaw or successful relief can break the encirclement. Circumstances decide whether a siege or broader operational blockade is in effect.

## 21.7 Maintaining a frontline and occupation corridor

A frontier is the evolving geographical boundary between secured, contested and threatened spaces. It follows effective access networks and strongpoints, not necessarily the historic diplomatic boundary. Fortresses control approaches only while garrisoned, supplied and capable of opposing intruders; their effectiveness is reduced by isolation, disrupted command, destroyed roads or hostile local conditions.

A continuous front is possible but not required. Isolated garrisons, pockets of resistance, contested rural areas and open gaps are normal. The player can establish supply corridors linking occupied towns and depots. If the corridor is severed, a forward army may remain present while losing reliable reinforcement or provisioning. Holding a road alone is not proof that all countryside on either side is controlled.

## 21.8 Political settlement and durable annexation

Defeating the enemy army and imposing occupation can force surrender or create negotiation leverage. It does **not** rewrite legal sovereignty instantly. Durable ownership changes through peace treaties, capitulation agreements, grants, succession arrangements, accepted allegiance or other modeled political transitions. Borders may follow geographic features, named districts or negotiated lines, but must resolve into actual locations and institutional rights without deleting existing settlements or landholders.

Annexation changes the authority that can claim lawful taxes, appoint officials and negotiate vassal obligations. Actual administration is still limited by compliance, infrastructure, political institutions and competing claims. A treaty transferring a valley can require defending troops to withdraw and occupying troops to hand over certain towns; these movements consume time. A frontier can thus be legally settled but militarily insecure for a period. Revolt and civil war may reverse de facto control without instantly changing every legal title.

Peace may also grant limited occupation, tribute, military corridors, demilitarized areas, shared access or fort demolition. These are explicitly encoded obligations with expiration, scope and breach conditions. Treaties never teleport populations, stocks or armies.

## 21.9 Taxation, markets and settlement life under occupation

Local economic entities keep their inventories, production assets, rights and obligations until an event actually transfers them. Occupation can disrupt markets, impose tolls, seize goods under a specific requisition policy, or redirect certain taxes where enforcement and consent permit. An occupier doesn't automatically gain an enemy region's maximum economic output; a legal ruler doesn't collect full revenue from a district cut off and held by another army. Taxes and physical grain may be collected once, not credited to both claimants.

Peasants need not always resist. Some communities negotiate local protections or shift allegiance to survive. Others hide grain, abandon fields or support defenders. Scarcity and blocked commerce can shift market prices and migration without scripted province-wide penalties. Armies maintaining control must be included in local consumption and logistics. Each consequence is grounded in stored goods, labor, passage constraints or an explicit political decision.

## 21.10 Player commands, maps and information

The campaign map exposes at least three distinct overlays: **political rights/borders**, **effective military control and contested ground**, and **supply/passage accessibility**. Additional overlays show claims, local loyalty, reconnaissance and siege isolation. Borders and overlays are spatially consistent but not interchangeable. Control should appear as secured corridors, threatened ground, pockets, frontiers and contested centers—not a universal expanding flood fill.

Available operational commands include `March / Transit`, `Request Access`, `Secure Area`, `Hold Crossing`, `Garrison`, `Patrol`, `Raid`, `Blockade`, `Besiege`, `Withdraw` and `Pursue`. These are intent categories rather than rigid UI labels; the client may present them contextually. Every command states eligibility, time, approximate forces required, logistical support, expected resistance, legal status, uncertainty and the likely consequences of abandoning the action. Unattainable control goals explain which routes or enemy strongholds remain unsecured.

The player sees verified observations, intelligence-based estimates and unknown areas separately. A control field may be unknown or outdated beyond scouts and garrisons. The interface must not reveal an invisible enemy simply because its true control influence is computed by the simulator. Summary notifications should report **why** control shifted: bridge taken, patrol withdrawn, fortress surrendered, army supply cut, or authority defected.

## 21.11 Relationship to tactical battles

A patrol intercepted at a ford, an army attacking a garrisoned road junction, a siege relief attempt and a breakout all create encounters at actual coordinates under Section 20's contact rules. The tactical map inherits the held bridge, defensive works, attacking directions, prior scouting, reinforcements and weather. Tactical victory changes local military capacity and potentially control of specific sites; it does not award a whole administrative region just because one battle was won. Retreat routes depend on the surviving terrain, opposing forces and permission/access conditions.

Autoresolve and manual battles must be consistent with the control system. Occupation does not require manual battles against every defenseless settlement, while actual armed resistance cannot be removed by a `Secure Area` order. Garrison capture, withdrawal, destruction of crossings and surrendered towns commit to world history and change the following operational picture.

## 21.12 AI, history generation and performance

Generated pre-campaign history uses the same abstract categories: sovereignty, rights, military control, contested marches, negotiated transit and durable settlement of conflicts. Historical wars can leave frontier castles, occupied corridors, claims and recurring border disputes. AI rivals plan conquest around routes, towns, bridges and supply, negotiate passage, defend chokepoints, try to relieve pockets and abandon occupations they cannot support. They must not receive free control beyond their real military presence or see hidden player forces through the influence algorithm.

The control model may use cached geographic accessibility, regional transport graphs, coarse patrol-capacity fields and local event-driven updates rather than a dense full-map fluid simulation every frame. The abstraction is acceptable if it passes scenarios for narrow passes, porous open country, hostile fortresses, allied transit, isolating alternate supply routes and persistent changes after battle. **Never replace military reach with automatic legal ownership.**
