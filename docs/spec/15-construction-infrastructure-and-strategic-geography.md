# 15. Construction, infrastructure and strategic geography

## 15.1 Three kinds of development

**Organic/private:** housing, ordinary farms, shops and small workshops grow where conditions and returns allow. **Encouraged:** crown or lord uses grants, privileges, guarantees, labor incentives and infrastructure to influence private development. **Direct:** crown or political rights-holder commissions and owns strategic forts, walls, bridges, roads, granaries, depots, ports, arsenals and institutions.

This division keeps the player from placing every cottage. A settled economy should develop even without frequent player clicks, though war, maladministration or isolation can halt it.

## 15.2 Construction projects are real processes

A project has a geographic site, chosen scale/type, legal authority, proposed design, required materials, workforce/skill needs, route access, treasury or other financing and phased progress. Project performance changes with weather, seasonal constraints, labor competition, danger, transport delay and design complexity. Build progress cannot advance when critical inputs are unavailable. An estimate is probabilistic and updates when bottlenecks change.

Ordinary projects run automatically after authorization. The player may increase funding, improve access, recruit specialist labor, pause work, reduce scope or cancel at sunk cost. Work already completed remains physically and economically consequential; unfinished projects may leave foundations, earthworks or usable partial roads.

## 15.3 Roads and bridges

A road is a real connected path placed in terrain subject to route grading, land rights, river crossings, engineering and cost. It has quality, drainage, maintenance condition, throughput and travel penalties for particular unit/vehicle types. Roads do not make army movement instantaneous. Repair and upkeep matter; military use can overload weak crossings. Road junctions and fords form natural strategic objectives.

Bridges have location, usable width, weight/load and possible destruction/repair state. Destroying a bridge in a tactical battle must sever or restrict campaign transport until repaired. A shallow ford can disappear under higher discharge. Tunnels or technologically implausible ultra-steep roads are not default solutions to difficult geography.

## 15.4 Fortresses and military installations

Fortifications have defensible physical footprints, terrain-dependent construction, walls/gates/towers/ditches, internal facilities, stores, garrisons, water sources and road access. A fortress's tactical scene is generated from its persistent structure; it is not a castle skin selected at battle start.

Player chooses strategic site and fortification type/scale. Architecture adapts intelligently to height, slope, rock, water, road and era, with limited meaningful layout preferences (gate orientation, wall circuit, keep position, ditch works and storage emphasis). Do not require detailed per-stone castle editing. A hill fort cannot be erected overnight, nor can impossible wall segments float above ravines.

## 15.5 Public works and environmental intervention

Irrigation, drainage channels, mills, embankments, logging tracks and waterworks may alter local land productivity or flow within engineering constraints. Such changes persist and can shift flood risk, water rights or soil conditions. Large modern-style dams and continental canal mega-projects are excluded from the historical scope.

## 15.6 Ownership and destruction

Projects inherit rights-holder ownership and jurisdiction. Capturing a town does not magically transfer every private workshop to crown domain; occupying forts and ports may transfer military control while civilian property remains contested. Fire, siege, flooding, abandonment and neglect can damage structures, with repair using real labor and materials. Ruins persist where relevant.

### Fortified corridors and maintained occupation

Roads, bridges, passes, ports, fortified towns, watchposts and castles are strategic control nodes. Their ability to secure surrounding routes comes from actual personnel, surveillance, mobility, geography and provisioning. An army that bypasses a hostile fortress can leave a dangerous threat to its communication lines; capturing a bridge can redirect trade and military movement without changing sovereignty over an entire province. Occupation infrastructure is a real economic commitment: camps and garrisons need pay, provisions, repairs, administration and roads. Destruction or evacuation changes control as soon as those capacities are lost.

## 15.7 Structures interact with the water, terrain and environmental atlas

A castle's placement, visibility and defensive slope derive from physical height and contours; its foundation and supply depend on local material stability, accessible water and terrain. A bridge is a persistent structure with real width, span, bearing capability, height above water, connection to both banks and sensitivity to flood/debris damage. Road construction changes the actual surface, traffic resilience and erosion/ditch conditions, not merely a movement multiplier.

Quarries, mines, walls, embankments, drainage systems, irrigation canals and human-cleared fields occupy real coordinates and create persistent changes in their associated spatial fields. Military and economic users must be notified when those structures alter passability, runoff, water access or route networks.
