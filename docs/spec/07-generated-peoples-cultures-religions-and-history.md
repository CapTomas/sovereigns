# 7. Generated peoples, cultures, religions and history

## 7.1 People and culture are not states

The world tracks distinct **population**, **cultural affiliation**, **religious community**, **political subjecthood**, **settlement**, **territorial claim**, **government** and **dynasty** concepts. They overlap but never imply one another. A kingdom may encompass many cultures; one culture may live under many rulers; cities may retain charters through dynastic changes; a conqueror may take a fortress without changing local identity overnight.

Culture emerges through historical continuity, language relationships, migration, intermarriage, institutions, prestige, trade, geographic isolation, and political integration. Represent cultural boundaries as distributions among settlement/cohort populations, not single rigid colored land cells.

## 7.2 Cultural traits and military practices

Culture records language family, naming conventions, architecture/art tendencies, inherited legal customs, food/crop traditions, diplomatic norms, and military traditions. Traits influence **preferences, institutions and accumulated competencies**, not biological superiority or rigid universal combat bonuses. A mountainous community may possess local route knowledge and experience fighting on broken ground due to its history—not an inherent innate “mountain people +15%” modifier.

Military traditions are learnable and transferable through warfare, training, mercenaries and political decisions. Conquest and prolonged exchange may produce hybrid equipment, formations and settlement architecture. Generated culture must not use real-world ethnic stereotypes as procedural shortcuts.

## 7.3 Religions and institutions

Religions are procedurally generated communities with doctrines, organizations, influence, local sites, clergy and varying relationships with political authority. Faith affects legitimacy, festivals, institutions, landholding and diplomatic relationships, but it does not cast spells or confer supernatural battlefield effects. Institutions may own property, operate granaries, administer settlements, educate skilled people, or contest crown authority. Conversion, schism and coexistence can develop through social and political history; avoid one-click total population conversion.

## 7.4 States, rulers and succession

Polities may begin as hereditary monarchies, confederations, city-centered leagues, principalities or other historically plausible governmental forms. The **player-facing selected faction is a kingdom-scale sovereign polity**, though rivals can have diverse institutions. Rulers, councils, noble houses and succession arrangements are meaningful. Ruler death may trigger negotiated succession, dynastic replacement, vassal realignment, rival claim or civil war. The player continues when the sovereign state survives; a change of ruler changes available relationships and obligations, not player identity.

Important people are individually represented if they wield meaningful power: ruler, heirs, generals, governors, diplomats, notable lords and religious leaders. The background population remains aggregate cohorts. Characteristics should affect judgment, political relationships and competence with realistic limits, not act as fantastical RPG-stat stacking.

## 7.5 History that truly happened

Pre-campaign historical events and played campaign events share the same concepts: settlement founding, migration, major construction, treaty, war, battle, ruler succession, kingdom formation or collapse, disaster and economic transformation. Key events retain date, location, participants, outcome, and affected world entities. The chronicle can turn these records into readable prose. It cannot contradict surviving roads, borders, ruins, cultural distribution or recorded states.

**Example causal chain:** volcanic/upland geology creates an iron-bearing district; a town grows along the ore-to-river road; merchants enrich it; neighboring rulers compete for road tolls; a fortress is built at the pass; the fortress is still on the tactical terrain centuries later unless destroyed. History is an explanation of existing world state, not flavor detached from it.

## 7.6 Generated names and readability

Languages produce plausible internally consistent personal names, settlement names, dynasties, geographic names and titles. Avoid unpronounceable symbol strings and names that collide constantly. Important places should retain names through time, with displayed alternative/historical names where conquest or cultural shift warrants it. Map labels must remain clear at multiple zoom levels.
