# 2. Campaign structure, player role and victory

## 2.1 Who the player is

The player controls the **continuing royal government / sovereign authority** of one political state. Personal rulers, dynasties and local institutions exist, can change and matter, but the player survives an ordinary ruler's death or dynastic change if the political state continues. It is not necessary to roleplay one character or accept character-based game-over conditions.

The kingdom can be centralized, fragmented or somewhere between. The player can command crown-controlled armies, negotiate with vassals, collect enforceable taxes and dues, issue laws within legitimate authority, direct royal spending, make treaties and appoint officials. The player **cannot** unilaterally extract every private asset or dictate every subject's daily work.

## 2.2 Starting a campaign

World creation exposes a short set of impactful choices: seed (editable and shareable), landmass/continent size, geographic character (varied / mountain-dominant / river-plains-dominant), prehistory length and civilizational maturity, and difficulty. **Default:** a large playable continent with credible regional seas, rivers, mountain belts and several mature but unevenly developed states. The generator also determines cultures, political borders, roads, cities, fortifications, resources, rivalries and historic conflicts.

After generation the player receives a readable atlas overview, world chronicle highlights, and a kingdom selection screen. Available states must be distinct in geography, institutions, military opportunities and problems. An easy/advanced description and starting strategic briefing should explain why they differ; a faction shouldn't simply be tagged “hard” without causes.

The player chooses **one existing kingdom**. The game does not begin from a vacant map or require founding a civilization from stone-age origins. No forced tutorial kingdom, but guided onboarding may optionally spotlight a suitable one.

## 2.3 Campaign time and pacing

**Design default:** one strategic turn represents **seven in-world days**. A turn consists of player planning, simultaneous orders by all states, and chronological resolution in smaller intervals (daily for most economy/movement, hourly near encounters and weather changes). The player can pause planning indefinitely; turn submission advances the shared world. Battles interrupt resolution at their actual encountered time. After resolving an encounter, remaining orders and elapsed hours resume appropriately.

The default has strategic tradeoffs: seasons last approximately 13 turns, campaigns span many turns, and administrative updates should be summarized rather than spammed. Provide **auto-advance with configurable stop conditions** (enemy sighted, encounter, shortage, project completion, policy crisis, diplomatic offer, important seasonal event), plus queued multi-turn orders. Prevent routine turns from becoming click-heavy. Speed tools must not skip an event that requires a player decision.

All factions issue orders for the same period. No special player-only time bubble. During a manually played battle, the shared world clock advances by the battle's duration; the rest of the strategic world is **not allowed to finish unrelated future days before that battle ends**. Resolve all state changes in consistent chronological order.

## 2.4 Scope of command

At kingdom level: diplomacy, laws, taxes, crown treasury, state-level projects, army creation, military doctrine, strategic priorities, vassal arrangements and regional administration. At regional level: governors, development directives, security, infrastructure emphasis, recruitment obligations and reserves. At local level: strategic placement of forts, roads, bridges, ports, depots and crown-backed productive sites where authority permits.

The player may delegate routine administration with transparent budget limits and policy guardrails. Delegation must not equate to the AI taking irrevocable high-stakes decisions without notice. War declarations, surrender, major treaties, seizure of noble land and substantial extraordinary taxes should require explicit player approval unless delegated by an intentional standing rule.

## 2.5 Victory and failure

**Primary mode: open-ended sandbox.** No compulsory 100-turn conquest deadline. Let the player define ambitions and witness long-term emergence. The game tracks achievements and measures of influence: territorial security, prosperity, institutional capacity, cultural influence, military reputation, durability, legitimacy and historical accomplishments, not only map-painted size.

**Optional campaign ambition:** choose a clear goal at start or later (unify a region, create a trade league, defend independence over generations, control specified trade corridors, dominate a sea, establish a lasting empire). These produce an achievement/legacy milestone, **not an involuntary end to the sandbox**. “Victory” should celebrate progress, never erase the world.

**Defeat:** loss of sovereign capacity when no legal territory, actionable political authority, recoverable army/seat or viable route to restoration remains. A state reduced to a vassal is not automatically defeated if it retains political identity and player-governable powers; the player may negotiate independence. Exile/restoration options exist only when supported by actual allies, claims, resources and rules, not magical respawns. The player can resign and view a final campaign chronicle at any time.

## 2.6 Difficulty

Difficulty primarily alters starting circumstances, opponent decision competence, information assistance, crisis frequency within plausible bounds and campaign objectives. **No hidden omniscience, free goods, impossible marching speeds or systematic battlefield-stat cheats** for opponents. Higher difficulty may make rival rulers more capable and reactive, reduce player-facing warning aids or choose more precarious starting states. All world rules remain shared.
