# 9. Land rights, nobles, institutions and decentralized government

## 9.1 Committed design: decentralized medieval governance

Choose the streamlined **decentralized** model, not unlimited centralized ownership and not character-heavy Crusader Kings simulation. Political power is distributed among crown domains, hereditary lords, subordinate rulers, autonomous towns, religious bodies, communal holdings and other plausible institutions. These entities have assets, revenues, obligations, preferences and enforceable rights.

The central design principle: **owning sovereignty does not mean directly owning every field or commanding every coin.** Kingdom policy affects the economy, but direct extraction and administration depend on law, agreements, local power and institutions.

## 9.2 Holdings, authority and titles

Every strategically meaningful parcel/site has an economic owner or customary rights-holder, jurisdiction, and applicable taxes or obligations. Some rights may overlap: a peasant household uses land, a lord receives rent, a religious institution collects a tithe, and the crown claims extraordinary military service. The model should support layered rights without needing to simulate centuries of highly specific legal minutiae.

Distinguish **landholding** (who benefits economically), **jurisdiction** (who judges and administers), **sovereignty/overlordship** (ultimate political relationship), **garrison control** (who physically holds a strongpoint) and **field control** (whose troops can safely operate nearby).

## 9.3 Vassal obligations

Vassals may owe defined shares of taxation, annual payments, military contingents, hostages, service periods, bridge upkeep or other duties. Obligations are negotiated and limited by institutions; they are not unrestricted army cards. A lord may send troops late or contest an extraordinary demand if loyalty, capacity or legitimacy is weak. Repeated overreach creates identifiable grievances and political risk.

When a vassal contingent joins an army, its soldiers and supplies exist physically; service-duration limits matter. The crown can negotiate longer paid service, hire mercenaries, reward loyalty or reform institutions.

## 9.4 Town charters and autonomous communities

Towns can obtain rights to hold markets, maintain walls, administer local justice, collect some tolls or negotiate fixed payments. These privileges encourage growth and commerce but reduce direct crown flexibility. Religious institutions may own farms, mills and land and act as diplomatic/social forces. Peasant commons and local customary access can affect land conversion and forestry.

## 9.5 Crown domains and reforms

The crown directly controls domains, arsenals, royal roads, fortifications, depots and other assets where rights permit. It can improve administration, standardize taxation, buy land, reward followers, negotiate charters, confiscate property under applicable authority, or centralize functions. These actions consume time, money, political capital and enforcement capacity. Reforms can yield powerful long-run benefits but provoke resistance from displaced elites.

## 9.6 Governors and administrative load

The player issues a **regional directive** (agricultural growth, transport, border defense, trade, revenue, provisioning or balanced development) and a budget. Governors/administrators carry out reasonable local actions within funds and rights. Their competence, honesty, connections and independence influence outcomes. They must explain projects, bottlenecks and failures. Administrative reach depends on roads, distance, communication, institutions and local legitimacy; huge empires cannot govern remote frontiers as cheaply as the capital.

## 9.7 Ruler transition, rebellion and civil war

Succession affects obligations, claims, confidence, factions and regional loyalties. Rebellion is not merely “spawn four hostile regiments”; dissatisfied authorities may withhold taxes, refuse levies, seize forts, form alliances, declare independence or start a civil war. Political conflicts use the **same geography, logistics, armies and tactical battles** as external wars. A civil war can split markets and disrupt production for both sides.

## 9.8 Player usability and scope

Do not require individual dialogue with every minor lord every turn. Show a **realm obligations summary**, major faction relationships and warnings about high-impact disputes. Routine obligations resolve automatically. Important concessions and reforms are deliberate strategic decisions. The game must remain Total War-like in command focus despite deeper governance.

### Territory and legitimate authority are separate from force

Landholding and political jurisdiction remain with whoever has the enforceable rights under the current order; an occupier may temporarily override local power but does not automatically become the lawful owner of estates, town privileges or noble obligations. A defeated lord can submit, defect, accept a negotiated settlement or continue resisting. Grants and annexations require a legal-political act and administrative follow-through. A claim of descent, conquest or protection can support diplomacy but is not self-executing. Civil war can create simultaneous rival claims, shifting local allegiance and multiple competing military zones without duplicating taxes, people or property.
