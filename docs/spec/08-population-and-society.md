# 8. Population and society

## 8.1 Population representation

The economy tracks **cohorts/households**, not millions of independently scheduled humans. A cohort's meaningful properties include settlement/location, population and age structure, livelihood/occupation, skills, wealth and holdings, culture/religion, health/living condition, political/legal obligations and availability for labor or recruitment.

Population groups must not count dependents as full-time laborers. Productive labor reflects age, condition, training, family responsibilities and seasonal allocation. Soldiers recruited from civilian cohorts reduce the available workforce; returned soldiers may resume livelihoods subject to injuries and disruption.

## 8.2 Population processes

Births, deaths, growth, aging, household formation and mortality occur through demographic update steps influenced by food, health, housing, weather extremes, war and economic conditions. Movement between settlements arises from wages/opportunities, security, housing, religious/cultural ties, legal restrictions and accessibility. Refugees from war or famine must go somewhere feasible, consume resources there and potentially alter society. People do not spawn merely because a house was built.

Population should react over **months to decades**, not jump dramatically every turn without a clear disaster, mass migration, conquest or other event. Displaced populations may be unable to relocate instantly when roads, security or seasonal conditions prohibit it.

## 8.3 Work and livelihoods

Represent agricultural, pastoral, extraction, craft/manufacturing, merchant/transport, service, administrative, religious, military and dependent populations. Occupational shifts need skills, capital, available work and sometimes migration. Rural households may combine farming, local crafts and seasonal service rather than being one permanent modern job title.

Work is autonomous within land rights and market incentives. The crown can hire labor, offer privileges, invest in enterprises or impose obligations where legitimate. It cannot simply drag unlimited people into arbitrary trades at zero cost.

## 8.4 Wealth and distribution

Track enough socioeconomic structure to distinguish poor households, middling producers, wealthy urban merchants, landholders, elites and institutional estates. Wealth affects ability to stockpile, purchase food, invest and survive high prices. The population's aggregate wealth is **not automatically royal treasury**. Economic shocks can worsen inequality and political stability without a universal happiness meter.

## 8.5 Food, housing, health and unrest

Households seek basic food, shelter, heating/fuel, clothing and local essential goods; richer groups may demand comforts. Consumption depends on available supply, local prices, income, reserves and social obligations. Malnutrition and disease increase vulnerability and mortality. Housing expands through local investment where materials, land and incomes permit. Unrest emerges from specific grievances: exaction, shortage, repression, conflict, broken obligations, religious persecution or disputed sovereignty. Provide reasons, leaders, locations and possible resolutions—not random universal rebellion percentage.

## 8.6 Culture and migration outcomes

Cultural change is gradual and contextual. Local institutions, conquest policy, language prestige, trade and mixed communities affect assimilation, persistence or new identities. Forced cultural uniformity is not a simple instant button; harsh policies carry human, economic and diplomatic consequences. Refugees and minorities retain social history and relationships.
