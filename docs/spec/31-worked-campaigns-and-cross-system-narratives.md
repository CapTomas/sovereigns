# 31. Worked campaigns and cross-system narratives

These examples are not cutscenes or scripted events. Each is a **test narrative**: the player or AI should be able to produce it through normal mechanics. Place names and values are fictional.

## 31.1 The river bridge that changed a kingdom

**Generation.** A continental mountain belt creates a wet western watershed and fertile lowlands downstream. Two sizable rivers merge near an easy crossing. Generations of trade create a market village, later a town. A royal state constructs a bridge and fortified customs post to collect tolls. The terrain, construction footprint, legal rights and stored economic activity all exist before the player begins.

**Peace.** The bridge shortens grain-and-iron trade routes. Market access helps the town grow and increases local land values. Toll income enriches the bridge's rights-holder but excessive levies can redirect traffic. Farmland near the river produces seasonal surplus; the town relies on grain and fuel deliveries.

**War begins.** An invading army targets the crossing to cut trade and enter the valley. Scouts report movement with imperfect confidence. The defender can march directly, detour to high ground, reinforce the fort or destroy the bridge. Every choice has path and supply costs.

**Battle.** Rain raised river discharge over the preceding days. The normal ford has become dangerous, leaving the bridge as primary crossing. Defender arrives early and places spear formations behind the bridge, archers on a hillside. The attacker can attack the bottleneck, attempt an upstream march or wait for weather to improve. If a battle occurs, regiments compress on the bridge, arrows follow wind and elevation, and soldiers accumulate fatigue.

**Aftermath.** The bridge might survive, be captured or collapse. If destroyed, caravans reroute and prices change in nearby markets. The successful general's troops retain casualties and ammunition expenditure. The chronicle records the event with the same coordinates used by the battlefield. After repairs decades later, the bridge can again become an economic center and a future battle site.

**Failure signals:** a river appears in tactical scene nowhere near campaign river; an unusable ford allows normal heavy wagon movement; the destroyed bridge still carries trade; the same army is replenished on return; a new unrelated castle appears at each revisit.

## 31.2 The expensive mountain fortress

**Generation.** A steep pass is the only practical wheeled crossing between two valleys. Rock is locally abundant but timber and food are scarce. A neighboring state has a long history of raiding across the pass.

**Royal decision.** Player selects a defensible rise overlooking the pass and commissions a fortress. Engineers design a physically feasible stone circuit; hauling material is slow and the local workforce limited. Improving the pass road might cut cost but also make invasion easier. A lord objects because the project affects their toll rights.

**Construction.** The treasury pays masons and workers; quarries provide stone; timber arrives along actual routes. Heavy early snowfall delays work. A smaller keep and granary complete before the outer defenses. The unfinished site remains meaningful on the map.

**Siege.** Enemy blocks valley access. The fortress's water source and food stocks determine endurance. A relief army seeks a second mountain route; snow closes it. Siege works change terrain and remain if battle transitions to assault. Garrison may surrender before walls fail if supplies run out.

**Long-term result.** A successful fortress stabilizes the frontier, attracts a garrison town and can generate a military road. But its upkeep strains a poor highland economy. The player can reduce garrison, invest in local food access or seek peace.

**Failure signals:** castle materializes in one turn with no materials; weather only changes the background color; walls appear as an unrelated tactical template; siege attrition is unrelated to inventories.

## 31.3 An agricultural kingdom at war

**Initial state.** Kingdom produces substantial grain but has limited local ironworking. Households rely on stable seasonal labor; the crown holds moderate reserves. A frontier dispute escalates at spring planting.

**Mobilization.** The ruler calls extensive seasonal levies, reducing available farm labor. Raising troops requires equipment from workshops and imported metal. The army consumes food and fodder. Merchants try to supply shortages along existing routes.

**Weather.** A dry summer reduces growth across susceptible fields. This year's harvest estimate declines before harvest day. Household demand, seed reserve requirements and recruitment withdrawals now strain the district. Rural leaders complain as crown requisitioning increases.

**Decisions.** The ruler may demobilize some levies, buy grain through ports, reduce taxes in kind, release granary reserves, or raid enemy supply routes. Each influences different population groups and future months. Rich households may afford imports while poorer ones begin rationing; unrest is localized and explainable.

**Battle.** If the ruler insists on campaigning, exhausted supply-poor regiments enter combat in a real location with inherited supplies. Their losses further reduce labor and equipment stocks. If the war ends, recovery takes time; crops and households do not snap back immediately.

**Failure signals:** taxes increase royal food without removing private stocks; demobilization has no effect on agriculture; imported grain teleports instantly inland; units receive unlimited resupply across hostile mountains.

## 31.4 The peaceful rise of a trade city

**Initial geography.** A sheltered harbor lies where two profitable overland corridors reach the coast. Fisheries, timber and nearby pasture support small settlements. Generated history brings merchants from several cultures.

**Development.** Traders invest in warehouses, a shipyard and housing; settlement population grows through migration rather than instant construction unlocks. Local rulers issue market charters. The city imports grain from fertile hinterland farms and exports manufactured goods. The crown earns revenue but must defend sea lanes and maintain the road network.

**Politics.** Merchants seek tax privileges; nobility wants customs dues; religious institutions establish holdings. The player negotiates. A distant war blocks maritime trade, forcing reliance on a mountain route that is seasonally risky. The city adapts or declines.

**Potential war.** An enemy fleet blockades the harbor at the campaign level. A land army can besiege the city, fighting a tactical land battle over real walls, quays, roads and hills. Dedicated ship-to-ship real-time battles do not appear.

**Failure signals:** prosperous city needs no food or fuel imports; changing rulers instantly removes local charter rights; naval blockade affects an unrelated generic percentage but not shipments and prices.

## 31.5 Three visits to the same battlefield

- **Year 550:** Forest covers the western ridge. A dirt road crosses a shallow tributary. A small village and ford stand in the floodplain. Both armies fight among woods and fields.
- **Year 575:** A logging program and settlement expansion cleared much of the ridge. A new bridge and farms exist. Combat is more open, cavalry has more room, and the bridge has tactical significance.
- **Year 620:** The town has stone walls. A prior war damaged the bridge, which was repaired with a narrower usable span. A local levee altered floodwater. The original ridge, river direction and valley remain identifiable; the terrain changes are attributable to world history.

This single scenario must be possible without hand-authoring three maps. A generated tactical location is persistent geography with evolving overlays and state, not a fresh die roll.

## 31.6 A loop around an enemy district is not annexation

**Situation.** A small enemy cavalry detachment gallops across open fields, circles a rural district and reconnects with friendly land. Its route encloses a town, a garrisoned fortress and several villages. The cavalry has neither occupied the town nor stationed patrols on its roads.

**Simulation.** The moving column threatens farms and may raid exposed travelers, but no enclosed-polygon event changes legal title or automatic occupation. The fortress and town remain resistant; the cavalry cannot enforce tax collection across the loop. Civilians may seek shelter and local prices react to danger. If the column leaves, the countryside ceases to be threatened once the danger is no longer supported by reconnaissance or patrol presence.

**Alternative.** An adequately supplied army occupies both bridges, the river port and all traversable mountain exits, maintains detachments and blocks known smugglers' routes. The district becomes economically isolated. Existing granary stock remains available to defenders until consumed; the castle may hold despite the blockaded roads. A relief army can attempt to reopen a crossing, triggering a tactical battle at that location. The region changes legal sovereignty only after surrender or an agreed political settlement.

**Failure signals:** circle closure paints instant annexation; unpermitted allied passage counts as conquest; a blockade ignores a viable sea or river connection; defenders starve without stock depletion; an emptied occupation corridor stays permanently secure without a garrison; the occupier and previous ruler both receive the same tax levy.

## 31.7 A single storm propagates through an entire kingdom

A maritime wind transports moist air toward a mountain range. Orographic ascent cools it, forming cloud and sustained rainfall. The windward foothills receive far more water than the dry leeward plains. Rain saturates a catchment's heavy soil; runoff reaches tributaries, and the main river rises after a travel delay. A trading ford becomes impassable for wagons, while a maintained bridge stays open initially. Shipments of ore and grain arrive late; market stocks change and prices respond to reduced deliveries. Nearby fields become waterlogged, delaying planting or damaging a susceptible crop. A marching army reaches the ford on the original predicted date but cannot safely cross. It chooses to divert toward the bridge, where an enemy has prepared a defensive position. The tactical battlefield loads with wet alluvial soil, a swollen channel, real crosswind, patchy fog and morning sunlight appropriate to its exact position/time. Cavalry performs poorly off the raised road while infantry holds the bank. The attackers destroy the bridge during their retreat, interrupting the region's transport network. Subsequent weeks see continued trade disruption until a real repair project restores the crossing. The scenario is invalid if any of these steps comes from an isolated weather roll or is forgotten on returning to the campaign.

## 31.8 One hillside through a century

A sunny lower slope has deep soils while the shaded high ridge has shallow rocky soil and persistent snow. Forest establishes selectively in viable patches. Human settlers clear lower woodland for rye and pasture, lowering local biomass and increasing erosion risk on exposed land. A road follows a gentler saddle, and a stone fortress is built above its crossing. Several winters later, snow and meltwater alter route viability; an army fights on the same ridge under different sunlight, frozen soil and wind. Its trenches and burned fields change the next season's local land use. Decades later, some woodland regenerates and the repaired road shifts settlement growth. Each difference is explained by stored or evolved physical, ecological and human state rather than regenerated scenery.
