# 10. Settlements, regional development and spatial economy

## 10.1 Settlement types and growth

Human occupation ranges from isolated farmsteads and hamlets through villages, market towns, major urban centers, monasteries, mining towns, ports and administrative seats. These are **descriptive developmental categories**, not rigid upgrades purchased with gold. Settlement growth follows productive surplus, market access, labor demand, security, political privilege, infrastructure and migration.

A village should not become a city because one building reached tier three. A city can stagnate or shrink if trade routes move, war destroys infrastructure or food supply collapses. New settlements can form near resources, crossroads, ports, river crossings or royal projects.

## 10.2 Settlement footprint and local land

Settlements have geographically situated buildings, fields, pastures, roads, forests, quarries, infrastructure and residential areas. Built footprints grow into adjacent suitable land with actual constraints: slope, water, existing rights, farm value, defensibility and roads. Expansion can displace land use and alter future local tactical terrain. A siege at a city must use its real river access, walls, streets and surroundings.

## 10.3 Land-use conversion

Land may be forest, pasture, cropland, orchard, settlement, managed timber, mine/quarry, marsh, water or other physically meaningful use. Converting land takes rights, labor, time, tools and often infrastructure. A cleared forest does not immediately become prime productive farmland: clearing, drainage, stone removal and soil preparation may be required. Poor decisions can cause erosion or flooding.

## 10.4 Urban specialization and dependencies

Cities concentrate artisans, services, markets, political institutions and defensive infrastructure but depend on rural food and fuel. Mining towns may depend on imported food, while river ports thrive on transport. Specialization emerges from relative advantage; it isn't permanently defined by a label. A royal charter or investment changes incentives rather than producing wealth from nothing.

## 10.5 Local security and control

Banditry, contested authority, raiding and garrison presence change accessibility and merchant risk. Local control decays away from defended routes and settlements according to terrain, manpower and institutions. An enemy occupying one town does not automatically secure every farm, woodland and pass around it. The political map communicates multiple kinds of control clearly.

### Occupied settlements remain economic and social entities

Military presence can establish security and extract compliance, but production, market participation and revenue depend on physical access, local cooperation, inventory ownership, the lawful or de facto tax collector, and intact institutions. A village might submit without fighting to avoid destruction; its farmland does not become royal property instantly. A town with walls, militia or a hostile garrison can refuse. Occupation can open, restrict or close roads and markets; adverse treatment can induce migration, concealment of goods, flight or rebellion. The state of each settlement is tracked independently from the surrounding countryside, and settlement garrisons draw real personnel and supplies.

## 10.6 Settlement and land use consume actual environmental state

The settlement engine receives spatially resolved **buildable ground, nearby water availability, seasonal flood risk, terrain stability, prevailing weather exposure, soil and field suitability, forest and quarry access, road connectivity, known ore deposits and climate limits**. An apparent opportunity must be grounded in actual state and institutional access. The same wetland, ridge, coast or riverbed used to determine settlement location also exists in battle, transport and construction. Settlement growth can change surface cover, runoff, timber stocks and nearby field use; it must not create a separate settlement-only geography.

A settlement's long-run development is constrained by productive land and environmental risk. Its inhabitants can adapt using irrigation, terracing, flood defenses, granaries, wood management and roads, with those works persisted as landscape features. An attractive place on a static map is not necessarily a good site in the active seasonal climate.
