# 6. Ecosystems, soils, resources and living landscape change

## 6.1 Ecology is generated from physical constraints

The visible biome is an ecological summary, **not a preassigned map color**. Landscape climate and seasonality, temperature extremes, moisture availability, soil, elevation, slope, exposure, fire regime, species distribution, animal pressure and human disturbance determine what can establish and survive. Plants have plausible environmental ranges and growth requirements. A short wet spell cannot transform a desert into rainforest; a persistent climatic change or irrigation project can gradually alter vegetation where biologically appropriate.

The simulation represents continuous cover/biomass and species groups at landscape patches, with dominant types and ecological transitions. Different biomes may intergrade. Riparian forests can follow water into otherwise dry areas; alpine grassland may remain above the treeline; wetlands occupy persistently saturated lowlands; steep exposed rock may support little soil or tree cover even under adequate rainfall. Tropical-like rainforest, temperate forest, boreal woodland, steppe, scrub, dry desert, heath, grassland, marsh and alpine ecosystems must emerge where supporting physical conditions exist.

## 6.2 Biological suitability is not instant vegetation

A place may have ideal conditions for mature forest but remain an open pasture because animals graze seedlings or humans repeatedly clear it. A burnt woodland does not immediately regrow to its pre-fire biomass. Recolonization needs seed source, dispersal, growth time, nutrients and suitable disturbance pressure.

Track enough slow ecological state to distinguish: current cover, potential cover, biomass/age, stress, succession/regeneration and harvesting/fire history. Dominant species/groups have their own tolerances and uses. Trees may be represented visually as deterministic local instances while forestry and ecological processes operate on real quantities at patch level. The current cover affects tactical LOS and traversability; long-term potential affects regrowth and local economic planning.

## 6.3 Soil forms the bridge between geology and life

Soil inherits parent rock and sediment properties and evolves through organic accumulation, weathering, plant growth, water movement, erosion, flooding and human cultivation. Track useful physical attributes such as texture, permeability, effective depth, nutrient/fertility state, organic matter, water-holding capacity, surface stability and degree of degradation. Surface wetness and deep root-zone moisture differ; a recently wetted thin surface does not guarantee full-season crop water availability.

Soil fertility is not a magic static number tied to province or biome. Appropriate crops draw nutrients and water; fallowing, crop rotation, manure, sediment deposition and land management can sustain or improve productive capacity; erosion, salinization where relevant, overgrazing and extractive cropping can reduce it. Recovery happens over plausible timescales.

## 6.4 Forests, grasslands and water feedback

Forests intercept rain, alter shade, shelter and near-surface wind, contribute to soil organic content and take up water. Grasslands and scrub respond differently to grazing, fire and seasonal drought. Clearing forest can open farmland and provide timber, while also changing local cover, erosion risk, runoff timing and future regrowth. A burnt forest can still have some standing trees and residual fuel; it isn't automatically a uniform empty terrain tile.

Biophysical feedback is continuous but bounded. Clearing a stand changes local infiltration/erosion and habitat and can alter drainage response over time; it does not instantaneously reduce rainfall across an entire continent. The ecosystem and atmosphere share field identities, with approximations scaled to processes they represent.

## 6.5 Natural resources are geographical facts

Mineral and quarry deposits derive from geologic structures, not settlement need. Forest products derive from living biomass. Fisheries and wild game derive from habitat and ecological productivity. Pastures derive from vegetation and climate. Fresh water derives from the hydrological network and accessible subsurface stores. A kingdom lacking convenient iron, timber or arable land must cope through trade, conquest, technology, adaptation or a different economy. Resources cannot respawn solely because a region is underdeveloped.

A valuable resource is not automatically useful. Extraction needs access, labor, energy, tools, legal rights, infrastructure and transportation. Geological reserves, known reserves, practical recoverable reserves and current production capacity are separate values; knowledge and technology influence the latter three, not the existence of ore.

## 6.6 Crop systems consume the physical history of a field

A field inherits real terrain, soil and water conditions. Crop choice responds to known species suitability, local culture, labor, seed, markets and household needs. A crop's growth state accumulates temperature/solar conditions and water availability over its actual growing period; waterlogging, drought, frost, heat stress, pests and nutrient limits alter eventual yield. Seasonal planting and harvest require labor at appropriate times; late rain can harm harvest or affect access even when annual precipitation is normal.

Irrigation can bring water to crops only from an actual water source with sufficient capacity and viable transport/engineering. Water diverted upstream can reduce available downstream flow. Drainage can improve a waterlogged field while changing nearby wetlands. Expansion of farmland replaces an actual land use and may consume woodland or pasture. Agricultural output enters genuine inventories used by population, traders and armies.

**Cross-reference:** Section 11 defines human farm management, crop selection, food reserves and obligations. This chapter defines the physical conditions those mechanisms must consume; it has priority over any simplified “crop category = output” interpretation.

## 6.7 Animals, disease and ecological productivity

Wild animals and fish are population/abundance aggregates linked to habitat, food availability, water, climate and human extraction; they need not be individually simulated outside relevant tactical/visual circumstances. Livestock need forage, water, shelter, breeding and care; they can damage crops, graze pastures, return manure, provide animal products or serve as transport and war animals. Harsh winters, drought and fodder shortages influence herd viability.

Disease and health pressures operate at an aggregate social/environmental level, shaped by crowding, mobility, food, water and sanitation. Do not implement fully individual pathogens or deterministic biome disease penalties. Epidemics and crop pest events require plausible exposure and spread conditions and explainable effects.

## 6.8 Human-altered hydrology and land cover are persistent physics

Roadbeds, quarries, levees, bridges, drains, dams of era-appropriate scale, canals, mills, ditches, cleared fields, settlements and castles occupy locations in the real world. They affect terrain access and sometimes local runoff, water distribution, erosion and vegetation. Changes must be localized to what the engineering and available labor could accomplish in the medieval technological setting.

Battles can burn vegetation, trample fields, create obstructions or destroy crossings. These effects alter ecological and economic conditions until recovery or repair. Tactical environment damage is submitted as world modifications, not discarded when the battle screen closes. A region regenerated later must contain the persistent aftermath.

## 6.9 Long-run cycles and limits

Forest growth is measured in seasons/years/decades, not instant build ticks. Grazing pressure and succession alter grasslands. Soil depletion may take many harvests; single exceptional floods may damage farmland immediately. Accessible ore, fuelwood, game and pasture can be exhausted locally. Degradation can cause migration and conflict; good stewardship and favorable circumstances can support recovery.

Long-run ecological summaries must continue updating in distant regions without requiring their every plant to be active. The game should maintain conservative aggregate biomass, soil and water history across unloaded regions, then reveal compatible local detail when the player visits.

## 6.10 What the player sees and what the agents must guarantee

The campaign map can show wooded ridges, fertile river bottoms, saturated marshes, dry plateaus, snow-bound passes and resource belts. The important part is not merely that these look right. Their **data must agree with their visual and economic consequences**: forest boundaries shape concealment and timber; soil and moisture shape crops; watercourses shape roads and sieges; snow and slope shape access. The player can inspect map overlays for elevation, watersheds, seasonal precipitation, soil moisture, land use, vegetation cover, productive suitability and environmental trends where their kingdom has adequate knowledge.

A biophysical change should travel consistently through the linked subsystems. Example: recurring dry summers deplete root-zone water, reduce crop yield, diminish forage, increase livestock-feed pressure, shrink taxable surplus and make armies harder to supply; nearby bare dry brush can also become more susceptible to fire. None of these should be independently scripted generic “drought penalties.”
