# 14. Money, taxation, credit and the royal treasury

## 14.1 Economy is not treasury

Distinguish **physical output**, **wealth held by subjects/institutions**, **government-accessible resources** and **spendable crown currency**. Taxation moves resources or money between owners under political rules; it doesn't cause resources to spawn. A kingdom can be agriculturally rich but have a weak treasury, or have a strong treasury and limited local food. Both states should be playable and strategically understandable.

Use a comprehensible medieval monetary unit in UI (a generic coin accounting standard) with regional currency exchange differences abstracted unless they matter strategically. Money stocks and transfers obey bookkeeping: receiving payment reduces the payer's funds, apart from explicit and auditable new coin production/minting associated with precious-metal supply. Avoid pervasive inflation mechanics that overwhelm warfare, but market prices should respond to scarcity and monetary conditions where meaningful.

## 14.2 Sources of royal revenue

Crown domains/rents, ordinary land taxes, dues in kind, tolls/customs, market privileges, town payments, mining/port charges, vassal obligations, tribute, war reparations, spoils and extraordinary levies. Collection requires legal entitlement, enforcement, local administrators, time and some cost; unreachable regions do not pay perfectly.

Agricultural dues in kind produce physical stockpiles at a real location. Money taxes require taxpayers to have access to currency or an institution able to mediate payment. Taxes reduce private consumption, investment and confidence to varying degrees based on burden and circumstances.

## 14.3 Expenditure

Armies' wages and provisions, construction contracts, bureaucracy, courts, diplomacy, garrison maintenance, public granaries, road upkeep, projects, patronage, debt servicing and emergency relief. Spending transfers purchasing power and hires labor; it cannot substitute for unavailable grain, stone or skilled labor. Unpaid obligations cause arrears, desertion, work stoppage, political conflict or credit problems as appropriate.

## 14.4 Policy design

Use clear **tax regimes by revenue category** (lenient/ordinary/heavy/extraordinary with appropriate numerical tuning) and explicit exemptions, charter terms and vassal contracts. Player can prioritize collection or remit taxes in disaster regions. Consequences depend on distribution, harvest, enforcement and legitimacy; no universal direct “tax rate = happiness” formula.

Important policy changes take time to propagate, especially across distant regions. Emergencies can override normal procedures only at real political and logistical cost. Regional advisers present projected revenue, risk and who bears the cost, not perfect certainty.

## 14.5 Borrowing and finance

The crown can borrow from wealthy families, merchant associations, towns, religious institutions or foreign backers according to trust and available capital. Loans have principal, agreed costs, due dates, collateral or political obligations. Default damages relationships, credit access and property claims and may trigger instability or intervention. Loans move existing purchasing power; they do not create physical supplies. Avoid simulating a modern central bank or sophisticated derivatives market.

## 14.6 Corruption and collection friction

Tax corruption, inaccurate assessment, inaccessible regions and embezzlement may reduce receipts. Tie them to administration, distance, official reliability and local resistance. Present diagnostic hints and corrective choices. The player shouldn't suffer unknowable permanent efficiency penalties generated from nowhere.

---

### Revenue under disputed control

A kingdom cannot collect a full normal tax merely because it has a legal claim to a region currently occupied by its enemy. Nor may the occupier instantly collect every historic due without administrators, compliance, access and enforcement. Tax and tribute flows respond to effective control of collection sites, local legitimacy, military pressure, competing authorities and the ability to move collected goods or money. Two rival governments cannot receive the same physical levy or inventory. Continued lawful ownership and the right to tax may diverge during occupation; peace settlements determine durable political changes.
