# 12. Natural resources, manufacturing and technology of production

## 12.1 Commodity set and purpose

The economy uses a **small, extensible set of meaningful physical commodities**, not hundreds of arbitrary crafting ingredients. Core groups: staple food, preserved food, animal fodder, livestock/horses, timber/firewood, charcoal, stone/clay, iron ore, usable iron, copper/other regional metals, textiles/fibers, leather/hides, basic tools, weapons, armor, construction materials and salt. Higher-value trade goods can exist without each requiring a massive bespoke micro-simulation.

Commodity distinctions exist when they change land use, logistics, household need, production processes, military options or strategic trade. Aggregate interchangeable varieties where detailed separation produces no meaningful decisions. A “unit” of a commodity must have an unambiguous internally consistent quantity/mass basis; the interface may show convenient units.

## 12.2 Production sites and chains

Mines, quarries, charcoal burners, mills, smithies, textile shops, tanneries, shipyards, farms and larger workshops use inputs, labor, skill, tools, energy/fuel, maintenance and output capacity. Output cannot exceed feasible input/worker/resource constraints. Some processes yield waste/byproducts and require water, wood or local access. A foundry without charcoal cannot produce full output simply because it was purchased.

Production structures can be privately or institutionally owned. They grow, contract or close according to markets, credit, rights, security and labor availability. Royal commissions can redirect output but compete for scarce materials and workers. Workshops do not automatically scale to infinite production because a war started.

## 12.3 Economic resilience

Inventories, substitution, reuse, imports, rationing, alternative recipes and delayed adjustments buffer shocks. Important substitution rules should be explicit (for example, different food staples can satisfy some caloric need, but not every resource can replace iron in quality armor). Chains should degrade gradually where appropriate rather than creating absurd all-or-nothing effects. Critical scarcities must still produce real consequences.

## 12.4 Extraction and discovery

Ore quality, accessible reserve and extraction difficulty derive from generated geology. Availability depends on discovery and infrastructure. Exploration/prospecting uncovers previously unknown economically viable deposits within realistic limits. Mining consumes accessible reserves and requires fuel, timber, labor, tools and safe transport. Newly “discovered” ore should not contradict the original geological world: it was unknown, not newly spawned.

## 12.5 Technological change

Production efficiency and product quality depend on accumulated skills, tools, institutions, available specialists, knowledge transmission and investment. Innovation moves gradually through contact and adoption; it can be faster in trade hubs and slower in isolated districts. There is no global abstract research point pool buying an instantaneous universal technology. The player can fund schools, guilds, artisans, military workshops and knowledge exchange; adoption requires real capacity.

Regional differences in arms, agriculture and construction arise from learned traditions, resources and institutions, not magic faction-only production multipliers.
