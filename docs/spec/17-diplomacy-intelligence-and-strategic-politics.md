# 17. Diplomacy, intelligence and strategic politics

## 17.1 Diplomacy grounded in interests

States seek security, access, trade, prestige, legitimacy and control of strategic territory. Treaties can include peace, alliance, trade access, toll exemption, military access, nonaggression, truce, marriage/recognition, tribute, vassalage, territory transfer, prisoner exchange and defensive obligations. Their feasibility depends on existing interests and capacity, not merely a diplomacy score.

## 17.2 Claims and reasons for war

Claims arise from actual prior rule, inheritance, treaty, frontier dispute, local allegiance, strategic ambition or religious/political institutions where relevant. The player may also make overt opportunistic conquest attempts, but suffers realistic diplomatic and domestic costs. War objectives guide AI settlement demands and campaign priorities. A treaty affects actual ownership and obligations only after enforcement/transfer.

## 17.3 Peace and negotiation

Peace may exchange territory, forts, toll access, reparations, hostages, vassal obligations, military withdrawal and claims. Terms must reflect bargaining power, political constraints and practical enforcement. An enemy cannot promise a nonexistent treasury amount or hand over infrastructure it does not control. Occupation and blockade affect bargaining without automatically ending war.

## 17.4 Information and espionage

Strategic information comes from scouts, patrols, travelers, merchants, allies, informants, watchtowers and direct contact. Knowledge has source, location, timestamp, reliability and possible inaccuracy. Unseen armies are not omnipresent icons. Last-known locations may become stale; forecasts and army counts may be ranges. Espionage may reveal intentions, sabotage infrastructure or aid diplomacy but cannot provide instant perfect information.

## 17.5 Fog of war by geography

Visibility and detection depend on elevation, line of sight, vegetation, weather, daylight, movement noise/dust, army size, road use and local observers. A moving army can be obscured behind ridges or forests. An observed crossing can alert nearby defenders. Opponents follow the same information rules; high difficulty is not license for AI map omniscience.

## 17.6 Trade and political leverage

Controlling a pass or bridge may grant tolls, deny supply or attract allied interest. A coast-dependent kingdom can suffer from blockades, and access treaties can restore commerce. Diplomacy has spatial consequences: permission for transit truly opens routes and withdrawal truly closes them.

### Military access, violations and postwar borders

Treaties separately specify military transit, duration, corridors, permitted force types and sizes where applicable, supply purchase, depots, basing, participation in war, and use of ports. Alliances do not silently grant every permission. A neutral power may grant transit without joining the war; an enemy of that neutral remains an enemy. Violating transit rights creates a known diplomatic offense and an ultimatum or crisis unless the receiving authority has already treated it as an act of war. A faction may intercept a trespassing army, demand withdrawal, negotiate access or escalate. Unauthorized passage never transfers land by itself.

Peace can transfer specified territory, castles, rights, claims, occupation status, passage privileges, tribute and obligations. The treaty must identify geographically coherent areas and actual affected settlements and holdings. Signing peace changes legal rights and creates demobilization/withdrawal obligations, not instantaneous teleportation of troops or a magical transfer of every local allegiance. Garrisons and remaining resistance still need resolution. The laws of passage, occupation and transfer apply equally to player and AI.
