# 19. Armies: recruitment, organization and supply

## 19.1 Combat scale and philosophy

Armies represent historically plausible **thousands to tens of thousands of soldiers**, organized into regiments/units the player commands. **Target presentation:** engagements involving roughly **10,000–40,000 total visible troops** under favorable conditions, scaled by world, battle and hardware constraints; this is a performance ambition, not a promise of individual full-physics agents. Smaller skirmishes and raids must also work. The unit of tactical command is the **regiment/formation**, not the individual soldier.

An army is a physical campaign organization: commander, regiments, manpower, training, equipment, morale, fatigue, supplies, animals, baggage, transport means, speed limits, location and orders. There is no arbitrary fixed “twenty cards maximum” rule. Command/organization capacity and logistics impose practical limits rather than a hard universal roster slot count.

## 19.2 Recruitment pathways

Professional troops, seasonal levies, vassal contingents, urban militia, retinues and mercenaries arise from distinct institutions. Every new soldier is removed from a real civilian cohort or an existing military pool. Recruitment consumes time, equipment, training capacity and political obligation. Demobilization returns surviving personnel with realistic delay, experience and injuries; dead soldiers do not reappear.

Seasonal levies may have maximum service obligations, changing willingness and civilian opportunity costs. Professional soldiers require ongoing pay and support. Mercenaries require recruitment networks, payment and contract compliance. Vassal troops have political loyalties and contractual time constraints. These distinctions create strategic options without requiring each soldier's unique biography.

## 19.3 Equipment and military logistics

Regiments carry equipment pools (weapon category, protective equipment, ammunition, horse/mounts where applicable, maintenance condition). Supplying a newly recruited heavy unit requires sufficient manufactured gear or imports. Losses and wear require replacement. Equipment categories are grounded in the available technology and military tradition. A depleted archer unit cannot fire unlimited arrows.

Food, fodder, water access, baggage and spare equipment determine effective field endurance. Army consumption uses **real, physically located inventories** moved via carried stores, local purchase, state depots and route shipments. No abstract infinite resupply inside friendly color-coded borders.

## 19.4 Unit families

Baseline recognizable battlefield roles:

- **Spears/polearms:** line holding, anti-cavalry defense, formation discipline.
- **Swords/axes and other close infantry:** close fighting, varying protection and tactical flexibility.
- **Missile infantry:** bows, crossbows, slings/javelins where culturally/technologically supported, with actual ammunition and ballistic behavior.
- **Mounted troops:** scouts, light cavalry, heavy cavalry, mounted archers where traditions and equipment support them.
- **Specialists/support:** engineers, sappers, siege crews, standards, supply and camp support.

Units are assembled from capabilities, equipment, training and doctrine rather than immutable faction-exclusive fantasy species. Named cultural variants convey real differences (equipment, training access, doctrine) without endless invented unit classes.

## 19.5 Regiment condition

Each regiment tracks manpower, organization, formation, experience, morale, fatigue, weapon/ammo state, movement, facing, cohesion and command state. Morale represents willingness to stay engaged; cohesion represents physical organization; fatigue affects performance; experience affects discipline and execution. They interact but remain conceptually distinct. A battered but disciplined unit can hold briefly; a healthy unit can panic under shock or isolation.

## 19.6 Commanders and command structure

Generals have plausible leadership, tactical preferences and reliability. Command limits and communication affect response delays, formation control and coordination, especially over large battlefields. The player still issues direct game orders; do not turn this into an opaque command-delay simulator that ignores clicks without feedback. Clearly indicate delayed, impossible or misunderstood orders.

## 19.7 Supply as a spatial network

Armies use actual supply routes over roads, rivers and sea lanes, from settlement stores, depots and transported shipments. Operational reach depends on carrying capacity, seasonal conditions, enemy action, available carts/animals and local resources. Occupying a town does not grant instant access to all regional supplies. Supply availability affects food, fodder, ammunition and replacement gear distinctly.

Requisitioning and foraging consume local civilian goods and may harm crops, health, trust and future tax revenue. They cannot create more food than the region physically has. Severe shortage leads to rationing, reduced mobility, desertion, animal losses and reduced combat endurance before mass death—rather than an arbitrary instant all-army penalty.

## 19.8 Army creation and disbanding UX

Recruit through settlements, estates, institutions or rally areas with transparent manpower, equipment, training and service constraints. Use army templates and automation for standard composition but never teleport ready units from across the map. Units muster over time and then physically assemble. Disbanding may release personnel and equipment, incur unpaid obligations, and affect local economies.

## 19.9 Weather and environmental exposure of an army

An army's marching needs and attrition are calculated from the same **temperature, wetness, terrain, daylight, slope, snowpack, flood state, water availability and route surfaces** used by villagers, crops and tactical battles. Cold and wetness can increase shelter/fuel needs; snow may close passes; hot dry conditions increase water demands; flooded rivers may delay convoys. Fatigue and movement depend on unit load, fitness, exposure and actual marching route, not on universal province weather penalties.

Army camps, foraging and repeated traffic can alter local ground, biomass and food stocks. An army does not carry a private weather bubble. When a battle begins, its fatigue, exposure and route history are passed forward as real time-dependent conditions.
