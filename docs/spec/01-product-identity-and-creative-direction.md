# 1. Product identity and creative direction

## 1.1 The game in one paragraph

*Sovereigns* is a serious, historically grounded-in-behavior medieval grand strategy game set in a **new, geographically credible procedural world each campaign**. The player rules the continuing political entity of a kingdom through rulers, crises, expansion and decline. Time-based turn-based campaign decisions take effect in a continuously evolving physical model of landform, atmospheric pressure and wind, humidity, clouds, precipitation, sun and seasons, soil water, groundwater, snow, lakes, rivers, ecosystems, resources, settlements, markets and populations. When hostile armies engage, the player can enter **real-time, large-regiment combat** on a detailed 2D orthographic representation of the exact location and current conditions. Battles feed persistent casualties, damage, movement, time and territorial consequences back into the same world.

## 1.2 Four design pillars

### P1. One coherent physical world

The world is a coupled, data-driven physical simulation: elevation and geology shape drainage, winds and sunlight shape weather, rain and snow feed soil and rivers, and those fields drive vegetation, agriculture, infrastructure and combat. Every coordinate has a consistent physical interpretation at every map scale. A battlefield cannot be an interchangeable random backdrop or an independently simulated climate.

### P2. Emergence without chaos

Geography and environmental conditions create incentives. Individuals, communities and rulers respond imperfectly. Cultures and kingdoms arise through simulated settlement, production, migration, conflict and institutions. Variety must be plausible, not arbitrary. The player must be able to understand why meaningful outcomes occurred.

### P3. Familiar strategic agency, deeper causality

The experience centers on government, diplomacy, development, operational maneuver and direct tactical command, rather than operating individual farms and workshops. Most economic activity is autonomous. Orders and policies let the player alter the world through intelligible, consequential decisions.

### P4. Strategic clarity over mechanical busywork

Huge battle formations, convincing terrain and meaningful strategic decisions must be communicated clearly through the 2D orthographic game client. Detailed visual identity and animation rules are reserved for the separate visual-direction document. Complex simulation exists to produce memorable choices, not numbers for their own sake.

## 1.3 Tone and setting

A grounded medieval aesthetic: austere, consequential, atmospheric, not comedic fantasy. The setting uses fictional peoples, places and histories that emerge from the world seed. Humans are the playable intelligent species; supernatural magic, monsters, spellcasting and fantastical resource chains are **excluded**. Fictional material may resemble a broad range of preindustrial societies, but should not simply rename known real countries. Religion, laws, warfare, social organization and technology are inspired by historically plausible medieval patterns, not forced into one uniform European template.

The intended broad technological envelope is roughly **high-medieval-equivalent preindustrial warfare**, with regional variation and gradual progress. No firearms, industrial machinery, electricity, modern state finances or industrial-scale logistics in the core historical era. Technology progresses through institutions, learning, diffusion and investment, not an arbitrary global research tree.

## 1.4 Gameplay promise and boundaries

- Single-player is the **committed** initial product format. Simulation structure should not gratuitously prevent future multiplayer, but simultaneous multiplayer, networking and deterministic lockstep are not design obligations.
- The campaign is **turn-based**, with **real-time tactical battles** that can be fought manually or resolved automatically.
- The world is **procedural-first**, including landforms, ecology, historical development and political landscape. Importing real Earth geography is **not** a launch mode and must not constrain procedural-first design; a common physical-world representation should make later import conceptually possible.
- The player rules a **kingdom/political state**, not a family, single ruler, abstract omniscient nation-spirit or individually simulated society.
- The player directs **regiments and armies**, not the routine activities of individual soldiers.
- The strategic game is not a city builder, survival crafter, grand-character soap opera or global logistics spreadsheet.
- Most information exists as state, uncertainty and geography, **not arbitrary static terrain-type bonuses**.

## 1.5 Player fantasies

Find an unknown world and understand it. Build a durable kingdom around actual opportunities. Create roads, bridges, strongholds and markets that visibly reshape geography and society. Recruit and train an army whose equipment, provisions and manpower come from your economy. Exploit ridgelines, bridges, mud, seasons and hidden approaches to win. Occupy the same valley decades later and recognize its changed roads, villages, woods and fortifications. Survive political transition and witness a unique world history continue.

## 1.6 What counts as success

A successful game session generates consequential choices with discoverable causes. A strategically valuable place remains valuable across economic, campaign and tactical perspectives. Repeating a campaign with a new seed produces materially different routes, state formation, incentives and battles. The player rarely feels compelled to click through routine administrative chores. Difficulty comes from meaningful constraints and rival intentions, not hidden cheat modifiers or unexplained randomness.
