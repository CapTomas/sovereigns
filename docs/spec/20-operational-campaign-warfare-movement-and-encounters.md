# 20. Operational campaign warfare, movement and encounters

## 20.1 Armies traverse the actual map

Army movement follows continuous terrain with roads, passes, fords, ports and navigable routes. Travel time is a result of distance, slope, substrate, road quality, ground moisture, snow/ice, army composition, baggage, animals, fatigue, hostile interference and command choice. Terrain penalties are derived from these fields, not region-wide fixed movement percentages.

Route planning offers **fastest**, **safest**, **least supply-intensive**, **avoid detection** and **custom waypoint** options, each with estimated time, bottlenecks and uncertainty. A path may be impossible for heavy siege trains though available to light infantry. The shortest distance may not be the fastest viable route. Orders continue across multiple turns unless cancelled or disrupted.

## 20.2 March postures

- **Normal march:** sustainable balance of speed, cohesion, supplies and awareness.
- **Forced march:** faster for limited periods; increases fatigue, stragglers, animal strain and supply consumption, often harming later battle readiness.
- **Cautious march:** more reconnaissance/security and lower ambush risk, but slower.
- **Concealed movement:** use cover, low-visibility hours, quieter trails and smaller visible columns; speed and supply options suffer.
- **Raid:** prioritized rapid attacks on vulnerable economic targets, with heightened hostility and limited ability to capture fortified centers.
- **Entrench/hold:** occupy good ground, improve local defenses and observation as materials/time permit.
- **Pursue/withdraw:** react to a known enemy while observing terrain, fatigue and contact constraints.

Postures create trade-offs through real mechanisms. They are not arbitrary buttons that add unexplained universal bonuses.

## 20.3 Armies occupy space and require access

An army has a physical footprint and marching column length. Narrow bridges, passes and gates constrain throughput and can cause congestion. Multiple friendly armies cannot pass through one narrow point at unlimited speed. Camps require water, land, security and space; winter quarters have different logistical needs. Armies can damage roads or trample crops if conditions and movements justify it.

## 20.4 Operational information

Scouts and patrols report observations; the player sees only what their network knows. Contact updates include last seen location, estimated size, heading and confidence. An enemy moving behind a ridge is not immediately rediscovered by magical range-circle detection. Knowledge of a major town's geography can persist without constant visibility; present territory and garrison strength may not.

## 20.5 How an encounter begins

Simply sharing a broad map region does **not** force a battle. A field engagement occurs when forces physically meet or deliberately intercept one another under movement, detection and initiative rules. Fast armies may evade; slower forces can hold chokepoints, cut escape routes or ambush. The moving columns' exact contact coordinates, headings, postures, time and prior intelligence become the engagement context.

The game must support skirmish, meeting engagement, defended position, ambush, relief battle, assault on camp, breakout, bridge/ford battle and siege assault. These are **circumstances derived from state**, not disconnected randomly selected map themes.

## 20.6 Engagement choice and commitment

On contact, present options where physically plausible: fight manually, autoresolve, attempt withdrawal, negotiate/parley, maintain standoff, or reposition within operational rules. Withdrawal has travel time and risk; once pinned against a river or blocked pass, a force may not be able to decline combat safely. Manual battle deployment is constrained by actual approach and preparation history. Do not offer a guaranteed consequence-free “skip battle.”

## 20.7 Multiple armies, reinforcements and retreat

Nearby forces can join if geography, orders, march speed and time make arrival possible. Reinforcement arrival has realistic direction and delay; armies do not teleport to arbitrary edges. A retreating army must select a valid route and may be pursued, obstructed or captured. Disorganized rout can scatter stragglers, lose baggage and create captives. Losses persist to regiments and population cohorts where appropriate.

## 20.8 Campaign positions and army splitting

An army may divide to scout, forage, screen, garrison or secure several crossings, but each detachment needs command, supplies and a physically valid route. Splitting weakens concentration and can allow the enemy to defeat forces separately. Rejoining takes time. Occupation, fort defense and blockade are real military tasks; a defeated field army does not automatically hand over all settlements in the region.

## 20.9 Strategic AI operating rules

AI plans routes, supplies, political objectives, scouting and retreat under the same physical limits and imperfect information. It should care about defensible terrain, operating seasons, road networks and force concentration. An AI cannot counter every unseen player maneuver through hidden omniscience. Its mistakes can be plausible and learnable rather than random suicidal attacks.

### Movement intent and control consequences

Army movement has an explicit operational **intent** in addition to route and posture: authorized transit, neutral passage request, hostile advance, raid, deliberate occupation/secure, screen/patrol, blockade or withdrawal. Intent is not a magical modifier; it changes legitimate behavior, interaction with inhabitants, commitment of detachments and diplomatic consequences. A fast march through an empty enemy valley may threaten the road but cannot instantly administer every village alongside it. To establish durable control, forces must actually secure settlements, crossings and local lines of movement, and leave enough capacity to maintain them. A mobile army can threaten a broad area while holding very little of it. Control changes are resolved in time as patrols, submission, resistance, garrisoning and supply evolve.

**Passage priority:** friendly own territory is traversable under own authority; allied or neutral ground uses explicit current permissions; enemy ground is a hostile incursion; unpermitted neutral land is trespass and may trigger interception or war. A treaty cannot make an army physically cross an impassable flooded river or closed mountain pass. Route previews distinguish legal access from passability, military danger and supply feasibility. Section 21 defines occupation, control zones and encirclement.

## 20.10 Operational terrain is the same physics at broader resolution

An army route traverses real slopes, surfaces, river crossings and climate-exposed passes. A flood rising upstream can change a route after the player issued orders, triggering an understandable reroute, wait, engineering requirement or dangerous crossing decision. Snowmelt may reopen a pass while rendering its road muddy. Army approach directions and deployment eligibility at contact derive from the same movement history and physical obstacles, with no arbitrary new valley generated for combat.
