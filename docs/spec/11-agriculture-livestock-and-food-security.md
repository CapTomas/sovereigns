# 11. Agriculture, livestock and food security

## 11.1 Agriculture as seasonal biophysical production

Crop suitability derives from soils, elevation, slope, local season, temperature, water, seed availability and farming practices. Each meaningful parcel or aggregate field holds crop/species, growth stage, soil condition, moisture, seed commitment, labor availability and predicted yield uncertainty. Accumulated warmth (growing-degree-like progress), water stress, cold, flood and pests drive development. The game does not require cellular plant physiology.

Crop choices are largely made by households/estates from local knowledge, tradition, need, expected prices, risk, seed stocks and access. Crown policy influences through incentives, guaranteed grain purchases, investment, seed support or coercive obligations, all with actual costs. **No player assignment of individual fields as routine play.** Strategic state farms and estates may be directed more closely.

## 11.2 Crop diversity and rotations

Support cereal grains (wheat, rye, oats, barley), pulses, vegetable crops, fodder, flax and regionally suitable orchard crops through data definitions. Not every crop is available in every culture or climate; techniques and seeds diffuse through trade and migration. Planting and harvest windows differ. Fallowing, crop rotations, manure and grazing management affect soil condition. Poor soil practices can yield short-run gains and long-run losses.

## 11.3 Livestock and pastoral economy

Livestock consumes pasture/fodder and labor and produces meat, milk, hides, wool, manure, traction and mounts where trained. Herd size responds to breeding, slaughter, disease, grazing pressure and winter feed. Horses suitable for military use are scarce trained assets, not automatic conversions of any livestock count. Overgrazing can alter plant cover and soil quality.

## 11.4 Inventories and hunger

Production is seasonal but consumption daily. Stored grain and other provisions exist in households, private granaries, lordly stores, markets, royal granaries, army depots and armies. Store spoilage, pests and preservation losses where meaningful. Supplies can be sold, requisitioned, taxed, traded, stolen or destroyed. All transfers preserve quantities; avoid magically accessible empire-wide “food points.”

Shortage follows a progression: prices rise, household spending changes, private reserves are used, imports sought, consumption reduced, nutrition and health suffer, migration and unrest increase. Famine is a **condition derived from access and consumption**, not a random event. Wealthy groups may survive food-price shocks better than the poor even in the same town.

## 11.5 Food policy and geopolitical strategy

Policy tools include public reserves, emergency release, trade access, tax-in-kind, wartime requisition, irrigation/drainage support, crop development grants and temporary import assistance. Each has fiscal, legal and logistical limits. The player can choose self-sufficiency, import dependence or export specialization—but terrain and institutions make choices differently risky.

## 11.6 Military seasonality

Recruiting agrarian labor during sowing or harvest can damage output more than recruiting in a slack season. Feeding horses can strain fodder reserves. Raiding can destroy crops or force harvest abandonment, affecting future production rather than applying a short-lived generic happiness penalty. Supply depots near harvest regions offer seasonal strategic advantages.

## 11.7 Physical production contract for farming and food

Every modeled harvest traces to **land parcel + soil + water balance + growing-season thermal/solar history + crop requirements + labor/tools/seed + management**. Cumulative rainfall cannot stand in for root-zone moisture; one warm day cannot complete an entire growing season; a crop cannot grow through unsupported winter cold. Irrigation requires a real source, flow/access and construction; drought, frost and flooding create crop stress through their physical conditions.

Field output enters locatable stores only after labor and harvest. A battle burning standing grain reduces the eventual harvest rather than destroying an unrelated decorative field. Flooded or compacted ground changes farming and army movement through the same environmental state. Annual or seasonal agriculture may use summary integrations of daily physical inputs without independently simulating every plant.
